## Definition

**Rapport**, also called a **pattern repeat**, is the smallest complete unit of a textile pattern that repeats regularly across a fabric. Once this unit is repeated side by side or one above another, it creates the full surface design.

A pattern repeat can appear in printed florals, stripes, checks, paisley, geometric motifs, jacquards, and woven designs. Understanding the repeat matters when a garment is designed, cut, or assessed because it determines where motifs fall on the body and whether they connect convincingly across seams.

## How Pattern Repetition Works

Every repeat has a defined height, width, or both. In a floral fabric, the repeat may consist of one complete cluster of flowers. In a striped fabric, it may be the full sequence of stripe colors and widths. In a check, it is the section that must recur to build the grid.

When garment pieces are cut, the repeat guides their placement on the fabric. A maker may align the design at the center front, side seams, shoulder seams, collar, or pockets. This process is often called pattern matching.

The repeat is not the entire visible print. It is the recurring building block from which the overall [pattern](/en/glossar/muster/) is created.

## Why Pattern Repeat Matters in Clothing

Pattern placement changes how a garment looks, even when the cut and fabric quality are otherwise identical.

A carefully handled repeat can:

- make stripes and checks look continuous across important seams
- prevent a large motif from sitting awkwardly on the chest, crotch, seat, or center front
- help pockets blend into the surrounding fabric or stand out intentionally
- create a calmer, more balanced visual impression
- signal thoughtful construction, particularly in tailored or higher-priced pieces

This is particularly noticeable with [vertical stripes](/en/glossar/laengsstreifen/), [horizontal stripes](/en/glossar/querstreifen/), checks, and bold graphic [prints](/en/glossar/print/). A disrupted line at a seam is often more visible than a disruption within a loose, irregular floral print.

## Pattern Matching at Seams and Details

Pattern matching does not need to be perfect everywhere. What matters is whether the construction supports the visual logic of the garment.

### Seams

On striped or checked clothing, inspect the side seams, center front, center back, and shoulder seams. Lines that continue across these areas usually create a more cohesive result. A strong mismatch may look accidental, particularly where the eye naturally rests.

### Pockets

A patch pocket can either continue the surrounding pattern or contrast with it deliberately. When the fabric pattern is matched, the pocket may almost disappear into the garment. When it is not matched, it becomes a visible design feature.

### Closures and center fronts

Button plackets, zippers, and openings interrupt fabric by necessity. With symmetrical patterns, the designer may place the center front carefully so both sides relate to one another. This is especially relevant for checks, mirrored motifs, and clear [placed prints](/en/glossar/platzierter-print/).

### Motif placement on the body

Large motifs attract attention. Their position can influence perceived proportion and [eye direction](/en/glossar/blickfuehrung/). A central flower, contrast motif, or graphic shape may become the focal point of a garment, whether intended or not.

## Large Repeat vs Small Repeat

A **small repeat** is often easier to cut and less demanding to align. Because the motif recurs frequently, small shifts at seams may be less noticeable.

A **large repeat** needs more planning. Pattern pieces may have to be moved further apart to ensure that motifs align or land in an appropriate position. This can increase fabric use and makes the placement of each garment piece more consequential.

Large repeats are not automatically better or more luxurious. They simply make pattern placement more visible and require more intentional decisions during production.

## How to Check Pattern Repetition When Shopping

Use this quick check for printed, striped, or checked garments:

- Look at side and shoulder [seams](/en/glossar/naht/) to see whether prominent lines or motifs connect.
- Check whether a strong motif sits where you want visual attention.
- Examine pockets, lapels, and plackets. Decide whether their pattern placement looks intentional.
- Open a buttoned garment slightly and assess whether the pattern remains balanced around the center front.
- Remember that an irregular allover print does not need strict seam matching to look well made.

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception: A rapport is the whole pattern on a garment.**  
  **Better:** The rapport is the smallest unit that repeats to form the full pattern.

- **Misconception: Every pattern must match perfectly at every seam.**  
  **Better:** Matching matters most for clear, regular designs such as stripes and checks. Some garments use mismatching intentionally, and irregular prints follow different visual rules.

- **Misconception: A mismatched pattern always means poor quality.**  
  **Better:** It can be a sign of reduced cutting effort, but it may also be a deliberate design choice. Consider the fabric, price point, garment type, and visibility of the mismatch.
