## Definition

**STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX®**, often called OEKO-TEX Standard 100, is an independent testing and certification system for textiles tested for harmful substances. A certified product has been assessed against set limit values for substances that may be harmful to human health.

The certification can apply to finished clothing, fabrics, yarns, and individual textile components. Depending on the product, testing may include materials such as fabric, threads, buttons, zippers, prints, and other accessories.

## What Does STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX® Mean?

In practical terms, the label tells you that a textile product has undergone harmful-substance testing under the STANDARD 100 criteria. This can be especially relevant for clothing worn directly against the skin, underwear, sleepwear, baby textiles, and sensitive-skin basics.

The criteria are not identical for every product. Products with closer or more prolonged skin contact are assessed under stricter requirements than textiles with less direct contact. This product-class approach reflects how a garment is intended to be used.

The label is one useful part of assessing [material quality](/en/glossar/materialqualitaet/), but it does not tell the whole story about a garment. Fabric weight, construction, fit, durability, and care also affect whether an item will perform well in a real wardrobe.

## Why the Certification Matters When Buying Clothes

Clothing sits close to the body for many hours, often during movement, warmth, and perspiration. For this reason, many shoppers want more transparency about dyes, finishes, and other textile treatments.

STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX® can make it easier to compare items when you are building a wardrobe of comfortable, well-considered essentials. It is particularly helpful when choosing:

- Underwear, bras, socks, and sleepwear
- Baby and children’s clothing
- T-shirts, leggings, and close-fitting knitwear
- Bedding, towels, and other home textiles
- Garments for people who prefer to avoid unnecessary exposure to potentially harmful substances

The certification should be considered alongside the garment’s [fiber blend](/en/glossar/faser-mix/), construction, expected use, and [care label](/en/glossar/pflegeetikett/). A thoughtful purchase is not defined by one label alone.

## How to Use the Label When Shopping

Look for the STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX® label on the product, packaging, online product page, or retailer information. Where available, a label may include a certification number and the testing institute.

Use the certification as one decision point rather than as an automatic reason to buy. Ask practical questions:

- Does the item fit comfortably and suit your needs?
- Is the fabric appropriate for the season and intended use?
- Can you care for it realistically?
- Does it work with pieces you already own?
- Is it likely to be worn frequently enough to justify the purchase?

For example, an OEKO-TEX certified white T-shirt may be a sensible everyday basic if its fabric feels comfortable, the neckline suits you, and it combines easily with your wardrobe. The same label does not make a poorly fitting or rarely worn item a good purchase.

## STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX® and Organic Cotton

STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX® and [organic cotton](/en/glossar/bio-baumwolle/) describe different things.

Organic cotton refers to how the cotton is grown under certified organic standards. STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX® concerns testing the finished textile or textile component for harmful substances. A garment can be organic cotton, OEKO-TEX certified, both, or neither.

This distinction matters because clothing labels can communicate different parts of a product’s story. Read claims carefully instead of assuming that one certification covers fiber origin, chemical testing, working conditions, durability, and environmental impact at once.

## Common Misunderstandings

- **Misunderstanding: STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX® means the garment is fully sustainable.**  
  **Better:** The certification focuses on harmful-substance testing. A broader assessment of sustainability also includes raw materials, manufacturing, transport, garment longevity, and end-of-life options.

- **Misunderstanding: OEKO-TEX certification means a garment is made from natural fibers.**  
  **Better:** Certified textiles can be made from natural, regenerated, synthetic, or blended fibers. The label does not identify the fiber content.

- **Misunderstanding: The label guarantees that a garment will be high quality.**  
  **Better:** Harmful-substance testing is valuable, but it does not replace checking seams, fabric density, fit, pilling risk, and overall craftsmanship.

- **Misunderstanding: Every OEKO-TEX label means exactly the same thing.**  
  **Better:** OEKO-TEX offers different standards and labels. Check the exact wording, as STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX® has a specific purpose.

## Practical Check Before You Buy

- Look for the full wording, **STANDARD 100 by OEKO-TEX®**, rather than relying on vague claims such as “tested textiles.”
- Check whether the label or retailer provides a certification number or further product information.
- Read the fiber composition to understand whether the item is cotton, wool, polyester, viscose, or a blend.
- Examine the garment’s seams, finishing, opacity, and feel to assess practical quality.
- Check the care instructions and consider whether you can maintain the item properly.
- Choose pieces that match your wardrobe needs and are likely to be worn repeatedly.
