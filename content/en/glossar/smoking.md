## Definition

A **tuxedo**, also called a **smoking** in many European contexts, is a formal evening suit worn most traditionally for [Black Tie](/en/glossar/black-tie/) occasions. It usually consists of a black or midnight blue dinner jacket with contrasting lapel facings, matching trousers, a formal shirt, and coordinated evening accessories.

The tuxedo differs from an ordinary dark [Suit](/en/glossar/anzug/) through its purpose and details. It is designed for evening events, where polished materials, restraint, and precise finishing create the appropriate level of formality.

## What makes a tuxedo a tuxedo?

The most recognizable feature is the jacket's lapel facing. Lapels are traditionally covered in silk satin or grosgrain, also known as rep. The jacket may have a shawl collar, a peaked lapel, or, in some styles, a notched lapel.

Classic tuxedo trousers match the jacket fabric and traditionally feature a narrow stripe along the outer seam. They are usually worn without turn-ups or cuffs. Together, the jacket and trousers create a calm, uninterrupted line suited to formal eveningwear.

A traditional tuxedo outfit also includes:

- A formal white evening shirt
- A black [Bow Tie](/en/glossar/fliege/)
- Formal dark shoes with a clean, polished finish
- Restrained accessories, such as cufflinks or a pocket square

Details can vary, but the overall effect should remain refined rather than businesslike or decorative.

## Tuxedo vs suit: what is the difference?

A tuxedo is not simply a black suit worn with a bow tie. While both are tailored outfits, their construction and dress-code role are different.

| Tuxedo                                              | Dark suit                                              |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Formal eveningwear                                  | Suitable for business, daytime, and many social events |
| Traditionally has satin or grosgrain lapel facings  | Usually made from one continuous suit fabric           |
| Often worn with a bow tie and formal shirt          | Often worn with a necktie or open-collar shirt         |
| Intended for black tie and formal evening occasions | Appropriate for a wider range of dress codes           |

A well-cut black suit can look elegant at a festive event, but it does not automatically meet a black tie requirement. If the invitation says black tie, a tuxedo is the clearest and most appropriate choice.

## When do you wear a tuxedo?

A tuxedo is appropriate for events that explicitly call for black tie, such as formal evening galas, certain weddings, award ceremonies, and selected dinners. It can also be relevant for [Evening Wear](/en/glossar/abendgarderobe/) events where a high level of formality is expected.

Always read the invitation carefully. Terms such as _black tie optional_, _creative black tie_, and _formal attire_ can allow more flexibility, but they do not mean exactly the same thing. For example, [Black Tie Optional](/en/glossar/black-tie-optional/) may permit a very dark formal suit, while [Creative Black Tie](/en/glossar/creative-black-tie/) can leave room for more individual styling within an eveningwear framework.

## How should a tuxedo fit?

Fit determines whether a tuxedo looks elegant or merely restrictive. The shoulder seam should sit close to the natural shoulder, the jacket should close cleanly without pulling, and the sleeves should allow comfortable movement.

Pay particular attention to:

- **Shoulders:** They should lie smoothly without collapsing or extending too far outward.
- **Jacket length:** The jacket should create balanced proportions with the trousers and torso.
- **Trousers:** They should sit securely at the waist and fall cleanly over the shoe.
- **Shirt collar:** It should sit neatly under the bow tie without gaps or visible strain.
- **Overall line:** A tuxedo should look composed when standing, sitting, and moving.

A precise [Fit](/en/glossar/passform/) matters more than an elaborate accessory. A simple tuxedo in the right proportions will always look more convincing than a highly decorated one with poor fit.

## Tuxedo styling for women

Women can wear a tuxedo as a complete tailored look or use tuxedo elements as part of an evening outfit. A sharply cut dinner jacket with matching trousers can create a confident, modern alternative to an [Evening Dress](/en/glossar/abendkleid/).

For a formal result, focus on material quality, clean tailoring, and an intentional finish. A silk camisole, a formal blouse, refined shoes, or understated jewelry can complement the look. The aim is not to copy a traditional menswear formula exactly, but to preserve the tuxedo's controlled, elegant evening character.

## Common misconceptions

- **Misconception: A black suit becomes a tuxedo when you add a bow tie.**  
  **Better:** A tuxedo has distinctive evening details, especially contrasting lapel facings and formal styling conventions.

- **Misconception: A tuxedo is appropriate for every formal event.**  
  **Better:** The dress code decides. Some events call for business formal, cocktail attire, or white tie instead.

- **Misconception: More shine makes a tuxedo more elegant.**  
  **Better:** Tuxedo elegance comes from restraint. One or two refined shiny elements, such as lapels or shoes, are usually enough.

- **Misconception: A tuxedo must always be black.**  
  **Better:** Black and midnight blue are classic choices, but the event, season, venue, and stated dress code determine what is appropriate.

## Practical check before buying or wearing a tuxedo

- Confirm that the event actually calls for black tie or formal eveningwear.
- Check whether the lapels, trousers, shirt, and accessories create one coherent formal look.
- Prioritize shoulder fit, trouser length, and comfort when sitting and moving.
- Avoid casual details such as sporty belts, casual shirts, or everyday sneakers.
- Let the invitation guide your level of formality rather than relying on the color black alone.
