## Definition

**Cufflinks** are small fasteners used to close compatible shirt or blouse cuffs without a permanently sewn button. They pass through the buttonholes on both sides of the cuff and hold the cuff together.

They are most commonly worn with double cuffs, also called French cuffs, but may also be used with single cuffs designed for cufflinks. Cufflinks can be understated metal pieces or decorative designs in enamel, stone, glass, fabric, or engraved metal.

## How cufflinks work

To wear cufflinks, fold the cuff back if it is a double cuff and align the buttonholes. Insert the cufflink through all layers of fabric, then secure it with its fastening mechanism.

Common closures include:

- **Bullet-back cufflinks:** A small bar rotates to lock the cufflink in place. This is a widely used and practical mechanism.
- **Whale-back cufflinks:** Similar to bullet backs, with a flat hinged piece that turns to secure the cuff.
- **Fixed-back cufflinks:** The decorative front and back are permanently connected. They can look refined but may take slightly longer to insert.
- **Chain-link cufflinks:** Two decorative ends are connected by a short chain. They offer more movement and a more traditional appearance.
- **Stud cufflinks:** Small, compact pieces that are pushed through the buttonholes and sit close to the cuff.

The fastening should feel secure without pulling, pinching, or making the cuff twist.

## Which shirts can you wear with cufflinks?

Not every shirt cuff is suitable for cufflinks. A standard button cuff usually has one button sewn onto one side and one buttonhole on the other. It is designed to close with its own button.

A cufflink shirt has buttonholes on both cuff edges, so the sides can be brought together and fastened with a cufflink. The most familiar option is the double cuff, which creates a folded, layered cuff and is often associated with formal tailoring.

Cufflinks can also be worn with selected blouses and shirts that have combination cuffs. These cuffs may include both a sewn button and an additional buttonhole arrangement, giving the wearer a choice between a regular closure and cufflinks.

## When are cufflinks appropriate?

Cufflinks are appropriate whenever an outfit benefits from deliberate finishing details. Their formality depends on their material, size, and design.

- **Business and professional settings:** Small silver, gold-tone, or matte metal cufflinks can support a tailored [suit](/en/glossar/anzug/) or a formal shirt.
- **Black tie events:** Cufflinks are a classic part of a [tuxedo](/en/glossar/smoking/) ensemble, especially with a formal white shirt and double cuffs. For [Black Tie](/en/glossar/black-tie/), restrained designs usually look most coherent.
- **Weddings and celebrations:** Cufflinks can add a personal note through initials, subtle color, or a meaningful motif, while still respecting the occasion.
- **Personal style:** More expressive designs can work well when the rest of the outfit is quiet and the cufflinks are the intended focal point.

Because they sit close to the hands, cufflinks become visible in gestures, at a table, or when wearing a jacket with sleeves that are correctly proportioned.

## How to choose cufflinks

Choose cufflinks in relation to the shirt, occasion, and other visible metal details. A small pair often has more long-term versatility than a large, highly themed design.

### Match the level of formality

For formal businesswear or eveningwear, polished silver-tone metal, gold-tone metal, onyx, mother-of-pearl, or simple enamel designs are usually reliable options. Novelty motifs can be suitable for private celebrations or relaxed occasions, but may appear less fitting in conservative professional environments.

### Consider metal tones

Matching every metal exactly is not essential, but the details should look intentional. Silver-tone cufflinks tend to work naturally with a silver watch, belt buckle, or [watch](/en/glossar/uhr/). Gold-tone cufflinks can connect well with warm metal jewelry or accessories.

### Keep proportion in mind

Very large or heavy cufflinks can pull a lightweight cuff out of position. Compact styles usually sit more neatly and are easier to wear under the sleeve of a jacket. The cufflink should support the [fit](/en/glossar/passform/) of the shirt rather than disrupt it.

### Choose a secure mechanism

Before leaving the house, test the closure and move your wrist naturally. A loose fastening can open during the day, while a rough or oversized back may catch on fabric or feel uncomfortable against the skin.

## Common misconceptions

- **Cufflinks work on every shirt cuff.**  
  They only work on cuffs designed with buttonholes on both sides. A regular button cuff usually cannot be converted without alteration.

- **Cufflinks are only for weddings or black tie.**  
  Formal events are a natural setting, but discreet cufflinks can also work in business dress when the shirt and setting are appropriate.

- **Cufflinks must be flashy to be noticed.**  
  Their effect comes from precision and placement. A simple, well-made pair can look more polished than an oversized decorative design.

- **Cufflinks are only a menswear accessory.**  
  They can be worn by anyone. The key requirement is a compatible cuff, not a gendered garment category.

## Practical check before wearing cufflinks

- Check that both sides of the cuff have usable buttonholes.
- Make sure the cufflink closes securely and comfortably.
- Keep the size proportionate to the cuff and shirt fabric.
- Coordinate the finish with nearby details such as a watch, ring, or belt buckle.
- Consider the dress code before choosing conspicuous motifs or bright colors.
- Ensure the shirt sleeve extends slightly beyond the jacket sleeve so the cuff detail can be seen naturally.
