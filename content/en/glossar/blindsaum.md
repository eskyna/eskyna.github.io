## Definition

A **blind hem** is a discreet [hem](/en/glossar/saum/) finish in which the folded hem edge is sewn by hand or with a blind-hem machine stitch. The stitch passes through the hem allowance and catches only a few fibers of the garment’s outer fabric.

As a result, the outside looks clean and calm, with little to no visible stitching. Blind hems are common in tailored clothing and refined garments where the fabric, line, and [silhouette](/en/glossar/silhouette/) should remain the focus.

## How a blind hem works

To make a blind hem, the lower edge of the garment is folded inward. The stitch holds this folded section in place while taking very small catches in the outer layer.

The technique is different from visible topstitching. A visible stitched hem creates a deliberate line on the outside, while a blind hem is designed to recede visually. It is therefore especially suitable when a polished, formal, or minimal finish is wanted.

A blind hem can be sewn by hand or with a sewing machine using a dedicated blind-hem stitch. The best method depends on the fabric, garment construction, and desired level of invisibility.

## Where blind hems are used

Blind hems are frequently found on:

- tailored trousers and [suit trousers](/en/glossar/anzughose/)
- skirts and dresses
- coats and formal outerwear
- garments made from wool, crepe, or other fabrics that benefit from a quiet finish
- occasionwear where visible stitching would interrupt the look

They are less ideal for every fabric. Very thin, tightly woven, shiny, or delicate materials can reveal tiny stitch points or develop slight puckering if the hem is pulled too tightly.

## Why a blind hem matters

A hem is a small construction detail, but it has a visible effect on overall quality. A clean blind hem supports a refined appearance because it does not create an obvious horizontal stitch line at the lower edge.

It also protects the intended [hem length](/en/glossar/saumlange/) and helps the garment fall as designed. On tailored pieces in particular, an uneven, twisted, or visibly stitched hem can distract from an otherwise strong fit.

## What to check when buying or altering clothing

When assessing a blind hem, look at it in daylight and move the garment slightly.

- **Check the outside:** There should be no obvious line of stitches, large stitch points, or repeated dents.
- **Check the drape:** The hem should lie flat rather than rolling or tipping outward.
- **Check the tension:** The fabric should not pucker along the hemline.
- **Check movement:** Walk, sit, and bend to make sure the hem does not pull or strain.
- **Check the inside:** The folded hem should be secured evenly and neatly.

The garment’s [fabric drape](/en/glossar/materialfall/) and weight influence the result. Heavier fabrics can often support a blind hem well, while fine materials may require particularly careful hand sewing.

## Common misconceptions

- **“Blind hem means the stitches cannot be seen at all.”**  
  A blind hem is not necessarily completely invisible. It is designed to minimize stitch visibility, but fine stitch points may still be noticeable on close inspection.

- **“A blind hem is always better than a visible hem.”**  
  Not always. Visible topstitching can be practical, durable, or stylistically appropriate, especially on casual garments such as denim. The right [hem finish](/en/glossar/saumverarbeitung/) depends on the material and design.

- **“Any hem can be shortened with a blind hem.”**  
  Not necessarily. The available hem allowance, fabric structure, lining, and original construction all affect whether a blind hem is possible and appropriate.

## ESKYNA Key Line

> A blind hem keeps the finish visually quiet, allowing the fabric, length, and silhouette to take center stage.

## Advisory View

For formal trousers, skirts, dresses, and coats, a blind hem is often a sign of considered construction. It is worth checking when assessing [craftsmanship](/en/glossar/verarbeitung/), particularly on garments intended to look polished and understated.
